First Passover, one winter, an unnamed feast AD 27 – AD 28

  1. TEXTJohn 2:13 “And the Jews’ passover was at hand, and Jesus went up to Jerusalem,”
    TEXTJohn 2:20 “Then said the Jews, Forty and six years was this temple in building, and wilt thou rear it up in three days?”
    GUIDE“This chart needs four Passovers between the Jordan and the cross. John names three outright — 2:13, 6:4 and 11:55.” That makes the first one spring AD 27. The guide doesn’t print this date; we computed it from the guide’s frame.
    GUIDEThe guide’s take-away test: remove “the tenth of Tishri” and “forty-six years still lands the first Passover.”
    CHECKOutside source: Josephus (Ant. 15.380) puts the start of Herod’s rebuilding in his 18th year, 20/19 BC. Forty-six years later is AD 27/28, which fits AD 27.
    CHECKThe weekday (Wed) comes from the fixed calendar. An observed-crescent calendar could move it a day or two.
  2. TEXTJohn 4:35 “Say not ye, There are yet four months, and then cometh harvest? behold, I say unto you, Lift up your eyes, and look on the fields; for they are white already to harvest.”
    GUIDE“John 4:35 … is the only clock between the first Passover and John 6:4, and it names one winter. One winter sits naturally in a one-year gap. A two-year gap means John named one winter and walked past the other. That is real, and the teaching does not pretend otherwise.”
    INFERENCEThe guide gives no date. If the verse is read literally, with harvest meaning the barley at Passover (16 Nisan AD 28 = Wed 31 Mar AD 28, fixed calendar), four months earlier is about Kislev–Tevet. Some read the saying as a proverb, not a date.
  3. TEXTJohn 5:1 “After this there was a feast of the Jews; and Jesus went up to Jerusalem.”
    GUIDE“The fourth would be John 5:1, which he calls only a feast. An earlier version of this teaching rested the ministry’s length on that verse. It no longer does.”
    GUIDELedger: “John 5:1 used as a weapon against AD 33” → “Withdrawn. It cuts both ways, and the manuscript evidence had been miscited.”
    INFERENCEThe debate: some manuscripts read “the feast” (with the article), which some take to mean Passover or Tabernacles. Others read “a feast”, and Purim and Pentecost have been proposed. Candidates on this frame (fixed calendar): Purim Sun 29 Feb AD 28; Passover Mon 29 Mar AD 28; Pentecost Wed 19 May AD 28; Tabernacles Thu 23 Sep AD 28.
  4. GUIDEThe four-Passover frame needs one here. The guide now carries it with two arguments, “and neither touches John 5:1”: the fig tree (Luke 13:7–8) and the six months between John and Jesus.
    INFERENCEThe AD 28 date follows from the guide’s frame (Passovers of 27, 28, 29, 30). The guide doesn’t print it. John is silent on this Passover unless 5:1 is it.
    GUIDETake-away test: remove “the fourth Passover” and “The ministry shortens and the Jordan moves a year — and the cross does not move at all.”

⚠ Where our computation disagrees with the guide
“Accept the 23 March crescent and the fourteenth falls on Friday 7 April.” A first sighting on the evening of Thu 23 Mar makes 1 Nisan = Fri 24 Mar and 14 Nisan = Thu 6 Apr. Friday 7 April needs the first sighting on the evening of Fri 24 Mar (moon 46 h old, easily seen). The sentence slips one day. The year is unaffected.
Conjunction “22 March AD 30, 17:48 UT (Gregorian)”. PyEphem gives 22 Mar AD 30, 17:49 UT on the Julian calendar, which is the guide’s own convention. In proleptic Gregorian it is 20 Mar. The guide’s other crescent numbers (22.0 h, 10.1°, 42 min) match ours (22.1 h, 10.1°, 42 min). Altitude: guide 7.7°, ours 7.5° (method-dependent).
“The fixed calendar places 1 Nisan a full day before the conjunction.” Fixed 1 Nisan (Thu 23 Mar) begins at sunset Wed 22 Mar, about 15:51 UT, roughly 2 hours before the conjunction. It is before the conjunction, but not by a full day. The point that no observing court could have produced a Wednesday still stands.
“Three years and seven months” for 1,302 days is a round-up: 44 Hebrew months + 4 days = 3 Hebrew years, 6 months, 4 days (≈3 years 6¾ months solar). The guide’s own figure of 44 months is correct.
Luke 3:1. Not a computation error but a break the guide itself admits: no attested count of Tiberius puts his fifteenth year in AD 26 (sole reign: Aug 28 – Aug 29).

What could not be verified
  • The guide says “Twenty-three such checks were computed. Zero failed.” It lists only six of them. We verified those six plus John the Baptist’s 177 days; the other sixteen are not identified.
  • The guide cites no source for its crescent altitude (7.7°), so we could not reproduce it exactly. We got 7.5° at sunset without refraction.
  • Historical terms (Pilate AD 26, Caiaphas AD 18–36, Philip to AD 33/34, Herod 4 BC) are the guide’s figures. We did not re-derive them from primary sources.
  • Daniel 9:24–27 is listed in the guide with “note where this teaching ends them”, but the guide never says where. It is left off the line.
  • The guide places none of John 1:29–2:1, 4:35, 5:1, 7:2, 10:22, 12:1, 13:1, 18:28, 19:42, 20:1–26, the ascension or Pentecost on its calendar. Every one of those placements here is marked INFERENCE.
  • Every Hebrew date before the 4th century is fixed-calendar arithmetic (convertdate, cross-checked against Hebcal on 36 dates). The real first-century months were set by sighting and could differ by a day or two.
